# Odometer structure of the Rule 30 orbit closure

Let (h(t)=f^t(1)in\mathbb Z_2), where (f(z)=z\mathbin{\mathrm{XOR}}((z\ll1)\mathbin{\mathrm{OR}}(z\ll2))). For each (m), let (p_m) be the least positive period of the orbit of (1\pmod {2^m}). Because (f\pmod {2^m}) is a permutation, the orbit of (1) is a single cycle of length (p_m) in its own finite invariant subset. The periods satisfy (p_m\mid p_{m+1}), and direct computation shows powers of two with increasing exponent (for example (p_m)=2^7 at (m=16) and (p_m)=2^8 at (m=20)); more precision is needed for a complete table).

For any integers (s,t),

```
h(t) == h(s) (mod 2^m)  iff  p_m divides (t-s).
```

The forward implication follows because the finite orbit is a cycle of length (p_m); the reverse implication is periodicity. Since the (p_m) are powers of two with unbounded exponents, equality of (h(t)) and (h(s)) in all finite quotients is equivalent to (t=s). More generally, if (t_j) is Cauchy in (mathbb Z_2), then for each fixed (m) the residues (t_j\pmod {p_m}) eventually stabilize, so (h(t_j)\pmod {2^m}) stabilizes. Thus (h) extends uniquely to a continuous map

```
H : Z_2 -> closure({h(t): t >= 0}).
```

The same finite-quotient equivalence shows that the extension is injective. Compactness of (mathbb Z_2) and Hausdorffness of the orbit closure make it a homeomorphism onto the closure. Finally (F(h(t))=h(t+1)), and continuity extends this to

```
F(H(z)) = H(z+1).
```

Therefore the Rule 30 orbit closure is topologically conjugate to the dyadic adding machine, even though (f) is not conjugate to an adding machine on all of (mathbb Z_2) or on the whole odd coset. The distinction matters: the closure is only the procyclic component generated by the particular seed.

The induced 2-adic metric is generally distorted. If (v_2(t-s)=k), then (h(t)-h(s)) is divisible by precisely the largest (2^m) for which (p_m\mid 2^k) (with possible extra divisibility at exceptional levels). This gives a nondecreasing modulus of continuity determined by the period profile (p_m), not necessarily (v_2(h(t)-h(s))=k+O(1)).

## Consequence for the center column

The conjugacy does not make Problem 3 easy. The center value is not a fixed continuous observable (phi(H(n))): it asks for bit position (n) of the integer representative (H(n)=f^n(1)). The observable itself moves outward with the orbit index. Odometer structure would make a *fixed* low bit eventually periodic in (n), exactly as the finite-quotient periods predict, but it says nothing about a bit whose position is (n).

More generally, an odometer can have periodic observables: (phi(z)=z\pmod 2) has period 2 under (z\mapsto z+1), and any locally constant observable factors through a finite quotient. Thus even exact conjugacy to an adding machine does not imply that every chosen output sequence is aperiodic. To obtain a counterexample with Rule-30-like row lengths, one would need an additional continuous embedding/observable whose integer representatives have width (\Theta(n)); the odometer theorem alone neither constructs nor forbids such an embedding. In particular, it cannot serve as either a sublinear algorithm or a lower-bound proof for the moving diagonal bit.

The period exponents are in fact unbounded. For every positive ordinary integer `z`, the highest set bit of `(z << 2)` occurs two places above the highest bit of `z`; the OR term therefore has that highest bit, and XOR with `z` cannot cancel it. Hence `bitlength(f(z))=bitlength(z)+2`. In particular `f^p(1) != 1` for every `p>0`. If the quotient periods `p_m` had bounded powers-of-two exponents, some common `2^K` would satisfy `f^(2^K)(1)=1 mod 2^m` for every `m`, forcing equality in `Z_2` and contradicting the bitlength growth. Thus the inverse-limit conjugacy above holds unconditionally for the seed orbit closure. It should not be confused with a claim that the entire 2-adic map is ergodic.
